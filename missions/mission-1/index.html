⚡ Atoms and ions

We have reached a scale far too small to see with our eyes.

⚛ What is matter made of?

Everything you can touch is made of matter.

  • 📝 Your desk is matter.
  • 💧 Water is matter.
  • 🧂 Salt is matter.
  • 👤 Your body is matter.

Matter is built from incredibly small particles called atoms.

An atom is one of the basic building blocks of matter. Atoms are so small that you cannot see an individual atom with your eyes or an ordinary classroom microscope. But scientists have developed ways to study how atoms behave.

Step 5

⚛ What is inside an atom?

Atoms themselves contain even smaller particles. There are three especially important ones:

  • ➕ Protons — positive charge (+)
  • ⚫ Neutrons — no electrical charge
  • ➖ Electrons — negative charge (−)

Protons and neutrons are found together in the center of the atom — this center is called the nucleus. Electrons occupy the space around the nucleus.

electron proton neutron

Protons and neutrons are packed together in the nucleus, at the center. Electrons move around the nucleus in shells.

💧 What about water?

The water in your cup is made of water molecules. A molecule forms when atoms are chemically joined together. One water molecule contains two hydrogen atoms and one oxygen atom.

This is why water is written H₂O — the small 2 means there are two hydrogen atoms.

So even though a drop of water looks like one continuous substance, zoom in far enough and you would find enormous numbers of water molecules.

Step 7

🧂 Now zoom in on salt

Table salt is called sodium chloride. Its chemical formula is:

NaCl

Na represents sodium. Cl represents chlorine.

But there is something interesting about salt.

Not quite atoms

In solid table salt, sodium and chlorine exist as electrically charged particles called ions — not as plain, uncharged atoms.

⚡ What is an ion?

Remember: protons have positive charge, electrons have negative charge.

Normally, an atom has equal numbers of protons and electrons — the positive and negative charges balance. But atoms can lose or gain electrons. When that happens, the particle becomes electrically charged. A charged atom or group of atoms is called an ion.

Sodium

A sodium atom can lose one electron. It then has more positive charge than negative charge.

A positively charged ion — 10 electrons around the nucleus.

Chlorine

A chlorine atom can gain one electron. It then has more negative charge than positive charge.

A negatively charged ion — 18 electrons around the nucleus.

Opposite charges attract — that's why Na⁺ and Cl⁻ are drawn to each other.

If a particle loses a negatively charged electron, does it become more positive or more negative?

Pick a choice and submit.
Step 8

🔨 Inside a salt crystal

A salt crystal contains huge numbers of Na+ ions and Cl− ions arranged in a repeating structure. The positive and negative charges attract each other — this helps hold the salt crystal together.

● Na⁺ (sodium ion) ● Cl⁻ (chloride ion)

So before you put salt in water, you can imagine it as a large organized 3D structure made of many tiny charged particles.

Step 9

💧 What happens when water arrives?

Now let's return to our cup. It contains both salt and water, and water plays a big role in what happens next.

A single water molecule — two hydrogen atoms and one oxygen atom.

Water molecules have regions with slightly different electrical charges. Unlike ions, which have full electrical charges, water molecules have areas that are slightly more positive and areas that are slightly more negative. Because of this, water molecules can interact with charged particles, such as ions.

When salt is placed in water, the water molecules begin interacting with the sodium and chloride ions at the surface of the salt crystal. The water molecules surround these ions and help pull them away from the crystal. This process is what we commonly call dissolving.

🧂→💧 Dissolving

Imagine the salt crystal like this — ions packed tightly together. When water surrounds the crystal:

salt crystal
→
each ion surrounded by water

Water molecules interact with the ions → Na⁺ and Cl⁻ separate from the crystal → water molecules surround (hydrate) each one → the ions spread throughout the water.

The salt has now dissolved.

Step 10

🤔 Did the salt disappear?

No.

The salt particles became separated and spread throughout the water. They are simply much too small for us to see individually.

Key idea

Disappearing from sight is not the same as disappearing from existence. The salt is still present — it is just distributed through the water at a microscopic level.

🧠 Solve the mystery

Let's put all of our evidence together. Where does salt go when it dissolves?

  1. Salt is made of matter.
  2. Matter is made of atoms.
  3. Different types of atoms are different elements.
  4. Table salt contains sodium and chlorine.
  5. In salt, these exist as charged particles called Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions.
  6. Water molecules interact with these ions.
  7. The ions are pulled away from the salt crystal.
  8. The ions spread throughout the water.
  9. They are far too small for us to see individually.

So… the salt never disappeared. It dissolved.

💻 Code it: Part 2 — Using code to calculate

Imagine you have two cups of salt water. Both look clear.

But scientists can describe a solution using characteristics that we cannot always see just by looking.

For example, we can ask:

  • How much salt is dissolved?
  • How much water is present?
  • How concentrated is the solution?

These measurements help us describe and compare the two cups more precisely.

One useful characteristic is concentration.

Concentration tells us how much of a substance is present compared with the amount of liquid.

Even if two cups both look clear, they can have very different concentrations.

This is where computation becomes useful.

We can give the computer our measurements and ask it to calculate the concentration for us.

🪙 What is concentration?

Imagine these two cups:

Scientists use the idea of concentration to describe how much of a substance is present in a certain amount of liquid. For this mission, we'll use a simple measurement: grams of salt per milliliter of water.

The formula

salt concentration = grams of salt ÷ milliliters of water

Step 15

PythonPython

The / symbol means divide. So Python calculates 5 ÷ 100 and stores the answer in a new variable: salt_per_mL.
